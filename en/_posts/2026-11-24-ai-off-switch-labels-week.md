In my last trends post I wrote that the slop debate was turning into a question of who checks the work. This week's stories point somewhere slightly different. Platforms are starting to give users **the option to see less AI-made content, or switch it off entirely**.

## Canva: a switch to turn off generative AI

[PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/11/19/canva-now-lets-users-turn-off-generative-ai-features/) reported on November 19 that Canva now lets both free and paid users turn off its generative AI features. Flip one setting and tools like Magic Write, Magic Design, Dream Lab and Canva Code disappear. Tools that don't generate anything new, like the background remover, stay.

There's a second setting too: one that pushes AI-generated designs down in search results. Canva tags AI-made items in their metadata, but not every item is tagged, so some can still slip through.

## SoundCloud: putting "AI slop" in the rules

According to [Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/11/17/soundcloud-ai-slop-crackdown/), SoundCloud updated its Terms of Use and Community Guidelines on November 17. A new "AI abuse" section bans mass-producing near-duplicate tracks to farm payouts, using AI to inflate plays, and presenting AI-made work as human-made. Accounts with an excessive number of uploads may face upload limits or extra review, and SoundCloud can now withhold or reverse payouts. CEO Eliah Seton wrote, "AI slop has no place on SoundCloud." Worth noting: making music with AI is still allowed.

## Deezer survey: 80% want labels

The same day, [Deezer](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2026/11/ai-music-labeling-deezer-ipsos-survey/) published a survey run with Ipsos across eight countries and 9,000 people. 80% said both fully AI-generated and AI-assisted music should be clearly labeled. 60% said fully AI tracks shouldn't sit in the main charts alongside human-made songs. The interesting part: 74% of streaming users believed their playlists had no fully AI tracks, yet Deezer's own data showed nearly 57% of people who joined in October already had at least one. Deezer said it receives about 90,000 fully AI tracks a day and plans to ship a setting to exclude AI music before the end of the year.

## The view from Slop

All three stories point the same way: label it, and let people turn it off. I think that's needed. But as Deezer's numbers show, people are already listening to AI tracks without knowing it, and some of those tracks are probably on their playlists because they liked them.

An "AI or not" switch tells you where something came from. It doesn't tell you whether it's any good. Turn everything off and the gems go out with the slop.
